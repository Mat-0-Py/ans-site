Definitions

  • A gas mark is a numbered position on a dial, not a unit of temperature. The chart gives the temperature each mark is conventionally taken to mean; there is no formula, and manufacturers differ slightly.
  • Between gas mark 1 and 9 the steps are close to 25 °F apart, which is why the Fahrenheit column is made of round numbers and the Celsius one is not.
  • The fan column is the conventional setting less 20 °C, the rule UK recipes and oven manuals use. Some manufacturers say 15 °C and a few say 25 °C — check the manual before trusting the round number.
  • A domestic oven's thermostat is typically within about 10–15 °C of the dial, and the air near the door is cooler than the air at the back. Converting to a tenth of a degree is arithmetic, not accuracy.
  • °C and °F themselves are defined against the kelvin, so that part of the conversion is exact — the converter above handles it, and will happily work outside oven temperatures.

How it works

To convert an oven temperature from Celsius to Fahrenheit, multiply by 1.8 and add 32; to go the other way, subtract 32 first and then multiply by 5/9. That part is exact, and the converter above does it in both directions for any value you type.

Gas marks work differently. They are steps on a dial that a chart maps to temperatures, so the only honest way to convert one is to look it up — which is what the table does. Marks ¼ and ½ exist because the bottom of the range needed finer steps than whole numbers allowed.

For a fan oven, drop the conventional temperature by 20 °C and expect to shorten the time a little as well: moving air transfers heat faster, so the same dial setting cooks harder. A recipe that says 180 °C without qualification means a conventional oven.
